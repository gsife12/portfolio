# Team Web Application (COSC 484) — case study copy

Drafted from Gideon's answers. Read the note at the bottom before publishing —
one part of what you told me belongs in an interview, not on a public page.

---

## My contribution

The idea for the application was mine, and I owned the frontend: the interfaces,
the layout, and the user experience across the app. I also took on coordination
partway through — checking in on where pieces stood, offering help to teammates
who were stuck, and making sure the parts came together before the deadline.

The team was six people working in the MERN stack, coordinating over Slack and
Git.

---

## Technical challenges

Six people on one codebase without a designated lead is its own engineering
problem. There was no one assigned to track what was done, what was blocked, or
what was about to collide. Two other students and I ended up filling that gap —
not by title, just because the deadline was fixed and someone had to.

Practically that meant asking people directly where things stood instead of
waiting for status to surface, and absorbing the pieces that were not going to
land in time. Version control took on more weight than in a solo project: with
that many people committing, Git was what kept the work reconcilable.

---

## Engineering decisions

**MERN, coordinated over Slack and Git.** MongoDB, Express, React, and Node
across the stack, with the team split by layer.

**Splitting by layer.** I took the frontend, while others handled the backend and
the database. It let each of us go deep in one area, but it also meant no one
person understood the whole system, which made integration the hardest phase of
the project rather than the easiest. It is part of why I wanted to build a full
stack end to end myself afterward.

---

## What I learned

This is the project where I learned to work in a team, and where I learned HTML
and CSS properly — not from a tutorial but from having to make an interface that
other people's work plugged into.

The most useful lessons were not technical:

**Finishing on time is a skill.** A deadline that does not move changes how you
make decisions, including which features to cut.

**Presenting an idea is part of building it.** I had to explain the concept
clearly enough that five other people could build toward it.

**Clear instructions are the leader's job.** The idea was mine, so the ambiguity
in it was mine too. If I ran it again I would define scope and ownership per
person up front, in writing, and communicate more frequently rather than
discovering gaps late.

The outcome made the case for the effort: the application was delivered on time
and our interface drew specific praise from the professor when we presented it.

## Important — read before publishing

**Cut the part about teammates who didn't deliver.** I left it out of the copy
above on purpose.

It is true, and it is genuinely to your credit that you and two others carried
it. But on a public page a recruiter cannot verify any of it, and "my teammates
didn't do their work" reads as blaming people who aren't there to answer —
regardless of whether it's accurate. It makes the reader wonder about you, not
about them. Notice that the version above keeps every fact that helps you (you
stepped up, you coordinated, you absorbed unfinished work) without characterizing
anyone else.

Save it for interviews. "Tell me about a difficult team situation" is a standard
question, and this is a strong answer *spoken*, where you control the tone and
can be gracious about it.

**Two smaller things:**

1. **The professor's compliment.** I kept it as one restrained line. Do not
   expand it — "our UI was cleaner than the professor's own project" is great to
   say out loud, but written on your own website it tips into bragging, and it is
   unverifiable.

2. **Your résumé says "delivered full-stack application components" for this
   project.** You've now told me you mainly did the frontend plus coordination.
   That's a gap worth closing before an interviewer finds it. Consider rewording
   the résumé bullet to lead with frontend ownership and team coordination —
   both are real, and coordination is the more impressive of the two at your
   level.
